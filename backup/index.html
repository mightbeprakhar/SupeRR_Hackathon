01 · Definition

What counts as pressure

Before every ball we estimate the batting side's chance of winning. Pressure is how far that chance is expected to move on the next ball. We divide by the average ball, which gives a Leverage Index: 1.0 is an ordinary delivery, and we call a ball high pressure at 1.5 or more.

This uses the signals the brief lists. Required rate, wickets in hand, balls remaining and the closeness of the contest all enter through the win-probability model, and none needs a hand-set threshold.

02 · Model checks

Does the win-probability model hold up

The model has two parts. A resource table, fitted to this data in the style of Duckworth-Lewis, gives the runs a side should still score from any balls-and-wickets position. The chase model is a logistic curve on how far the target sits from those expected runs. Batting-first win probability is the same chase model averaged over the range of totals the side is on course for, so both innings share one currency.

Accuracy

Lower Brier score and higher AUC are better. The first draft is an earlier build of this project.

Batting-first win probability is weakly predictable by nature: the result is still 30 overs away. Its AUC of 0.69 is expected, and its calibration is what matters. It is less reliable at the extremes (above 90% or below 10%), where few matches sit.

07 · What changed

What changed from the first draft

This project started from an earlier draft of the analysis. We kept its data handling, which checked out, and replaced its scoring. The main reason: 30 to 35% of its score rewarded doing better under pressure than one's own baseline, and that difference does not repeat between halves of a player's matches.

10 · Method

Method and limits

How a player is rated

Scope.

Value of a ball. Runs scored or conceded against the average for the same format, innings, phase, wickets-in-hand band and required-rate band, plus a wicket value. The wicket value is the number of runs the win-probability model treats as equal to losing a wicket: .

Player model. Each player has a baseline level on all live balls and a separate pressure adjustment on high-pressure balls, estimated together with an effect for every opponent he faced. Small samples are pulled toward average by an amount the data sets. Run value and wicket value are modelled separately because run-scoring repeats far better than dismissals.

Rank. The 10th percentile of the estimated pressure value, which we call the floor. It answers the brief's word, reliable: high expected value with enough evidence behind it.

Dead balls. Chase deliveries with win probability under 2% or over 98% are left out of baselines.

Where the model is less certain

Pressure sits in the chase. On one win-probability scale almost no first-innings ball reaches 1.5 times average. First-innings death bowling is therefore outside the main definition. The robustness section includes a both-innings definition and a rule-based one that counts first-innings death overs.

Team strength is not in the model. Win probability reads the scoreboard only. Opponent quality is handled in the player model, where every ball is adjusted for who bowled or faced it.

Wicket values early in an innings may be high. Early wickets also signal hard batting conditions, which the model cannot separate from the cost of the wicket itself. Halving the wicket value is one of the robustness runs.

Samples are small. The typical ranked player has a few hundred pressure balls. Each rating carries a margin shown in the player panel, and neighbours in the list are often not separable.

Rain-affected chases. Four chases shortened without a supplied revised target are excluded. Other revised targets are used as supplied.

Data checks. Rebuilt innings totals match the official result margin in 790 of 793 decided matches. Treating the extras type "other" as a no-ball yields six legal balls in every completed over but one.
